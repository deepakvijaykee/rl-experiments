# rl-experiments

Six connected pieces of code for studying what RL update rules actually do to a policy. The base is [`rl_sandbox/`](rl_sandbox/), a small PyTorch sandbox of bandit and sequence tasks with a registry of update rules to compare on them. Each of the five companions exists because some method broke an assumption the scalar sandbox is built on, and accommodating it inside the sandbox would have buried the part that made the method worth studying. [`learnability_sandbox/`](learnability_sandbox/) works one step upstream of every update rule, at the boundary where a policy, an environment, and a rollout budget either produce usable reward contrast or leave the optimizer with no task direction at all. [`rlm_grpo/`](rlm_grpo/) trains small causal LMs (0.5-0.6B) with GRPO on a tree-of-rollouts protocol. [`opd_sandbox/`](opd_sandbox/) is an appendix probing on-policy distillation mechanics on similar toy tasks. [`vpo_sandbox/`](vpo_sandbox/) isolates Vector Policy Optimization on a set-valued, vector-reward toy, where the question is whether training should preserve a pool of trade-offs for test-time search or collapse onto one answer. [`pedagogy_sandbox/`](pedagogy_sandbox/) isolates Pedagogical RL as a privileged rollout-acquisition protocol with separate teacher and student policies.

The question driving all six is older than any particular objective family. When the reward signal is sparse, noisy, delayed, privileged, or vector-valued, which rollouts deserve gradient credit, how should they be found, and at what granularity should a policy spend its update budget? A benchmark answers none of that. It ranks methods by who finished first, which leaves you knowing that one method got there and another stalled without any account of why, and with no way to tell whether a winner at small scale will still win at a larger one. So I went looking at the training step itself: where gradient mass concentrates as the policy moves, when the importance ratio drifts past the band the clipped surrogate tracks, at what step entropy collapses past recovery, when privileged information produces learnable rollouts instead of merely correct ones, and when a policy has quietly collapsed a useful candidate pool into a single scalar compromise. Every one of those signals gets averaged away by distributed rollouts and large batches, so at scale the failure only surfaces after days of compute have gone into it. A single GPU with toy models and short horizons brings them back into view, and the rest of this setup exists to keep them readable.

## How I think about the space of choices

Post-training RL papers usually organize themselves by objective family: PPO, GRPO, DPO, RLVR, rejection sampling, distillation. After spending enough time reading them, that framing started to feel too coarse for the decisions I actually had to make. Two methods inside the same family can disagree about the choice that determines the run, and two methods from very different lineages can agree on it. The framing that has been more useful to me sits a level below the objective.

> Given a fixed budget of online rollouts, learner updates, and KL distance from the reference policy, which samples should receive gradient weight, at what granularity, and for what downstream use of the candidate pool?

The question splits into seven axes, and they fall in an order that follows how a training run actually unfolds. The first two take the collected reward signal as given and ask how to spend it: which samples get weight, and at what granularity. The third asks how long that signal stays valid as the policy drifts away from the one that collected it. The fourth asks whether the reward function deserved trust in the first place. The fifth picks up whatever the previous four have handed the optimizer. The last two step outside the update entirely, the sixth asking how privileged information should find rollouts the student cannot yet reach on its own, and the seventh asking whether training should produce a single answer or a searchable set of alternatives. Each axis is also a failure mode I can reproduce cheaply, which is the whole reason I can stare at the gradient when one of them goes wrong.

**Influence allocation** comes first. Every rollout arrives with an advantage, a surprisal, and a sampled action, and turning those three into a per-sample weight is where the first real decision gets made. Uniform weighting anchors the axis as the no-effect baseline, advantage-only weighting is the standard policy-gradient choice, and the delight family (DG and Kondo, from Delightful Policy Gradient, [arXiv:2603.14608](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.14608)) is the third bet. The usual shorthand for that family, "advantage times surprisal weighting," names the wrong object. That product is never the weight. It is the argument to a sigmoid gate, and the gate multiplies the advantage, so the effective weight is $\operatorname{sigmoid}(\text{advantage} \cdot \text{surprisal} / \eta) \cdot \text{advantage}$. Surprisal is never negative, so the product carries the sign of the advantage, and the gate treats the two signs asymmetrically. A surprising success saturates the gate toward one and collects full credit, a surprising failure drives it toward zero and gets suppressed, and both unsurprising cases sit near one half. What the family bets on, then, is that a policy should learn hard from lucky wins and discount unlucky losses.

GRPO sits on this same axis despite the different vocabulary, since its group-normalized advantage is one more choice of weighting. TPO is the one method that escapes the axis, because it never reweights a sample-driven update at all. It changes the update target, treating the rollouts in a group as candidates for a soft target distribution instead of as one sampled action and seven noise reducers. In this framing, then, the question to put to TPO is whether its candidate-target construction extracts more from the same grouped rollouts than GRPO's normalization step does. Whose weighting wins is the wrong comparison, because only one of the two is weighting anything.

**Credit granularity** is what an update faces next. Even with the weighting scheme fixed, an update still has to attribute the reward somewhere, either to the whole trajectory, to a branch within a tree of rollouts, or to individual tokens. Sparse-reward tasks need a credit story even when the feedback is a single bit, because flattening one bit across an entire trajectory drowns the meaningful tokens in noise from the unmeaningful ones. Dense-reward tasks have the opposite problem, where collapsing positions that each carry per-step signal back into a single sequence-level score throws away most of what the reward function was telling you. TPOToken and DGToken are the per-token bets in this sandbox, and `masked_reversal` is the task built to expose their trade-offs, since it gives the comparison both scored and unscored positions to read.

**Support and coverage** turns on how long logged data stays informative for a policy that has since moved on. Replay reduces gradient variance by recycling samples across optimizer steps, but every recycled sample comes from a policy that has since moved, and the importance ratios meant to correct for that mismatch are themselves noisy. A rollout collected k steps ago is approximately on-policy only while the policy has not moved much in k steps, which makes the useful capacity of any replay buffer whatever fits inside that freshness window. Outside the window, the gradient points at a policy that no longer exists. ReplayDG and FreshDG are the diagnostics on this axis, and the runs put the controlling variable at the effective age distribution of the buffer, with capacity mattering only through the age distribution it happens to produce.

**Reward uncertainty** becomes load-bearing whenever the reward is a proxy, such as a verifier, a judge, or a process reward model. Any proxy makes mistakes: some fraction of its high signals are false positives, and some fraction of its low signals are missed credit. This axis is unusually clean to study in the sandbox because three of its methods share a single uncertainty signal, the within-group reward standard deviation, and differ only in where they inject it. UncertaintyDG subtracts it inside the gate argument, which shifts the gate's threshold. RewardVarianceDG divides the advantage by it, shrinking the magnitude before the gate ever sees it. FilteredDG applies a hard keep-or-drop mask outside the gate. One signal, three insertion points, three different failure modes, and holding the signal fixed is what lets the failures be attributed to the insertion point at all.

ASPO and R2VPO put a different question to the same axis, and they answer it in opposite ways, so the sandbox keeps them together. ASPO ([arXiv:2510.06062](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.06062)) inverts the importance ratio for positive-advantage tokens, so a rare good action attracts more gradient instead of less, with one-sided clipping to keep the inversion from running away. R2VPO ([arXiv:2601.03320](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03320)) drops hard clipping altogether in favor of a penalty on ratio variance, which preserves the gradient of a rare high-advantage sample where a clip boundary would have zeroed it. DG and ASPO therefore amplify rare breakthroughs, R2VPO only declines to destroy them, and the comparison asks whether amplification earns its keep or preservation already suffices.

The three gates and the two ratio methods fail in different ways, and the noise sweep makes visible what they nonetheless have in common. Every method that downweights the update when the reward looks uncertain is choosing an operating point on the same true-versus-false-positive trade-off. Nothing on this axis filters for free. The methods differ only in where they sit on the curve.

**Optimization geometry** is where all of that lands, covering clipping, ratio variance, normalization, KL terms, entropy penalties, and the way all of these interact. The wrong combination can collapse entropy inside a hundred steps while the loss curve still looks healthy, and that healthy-looking loss curve is exactly what makes the failure so expensive to find at scale, since nothing in the usual dashboard tells you to go looking. The entropy-collapse sweep is the cheapest setting I know of for watching it happen, and it points clearly at which part of the GRPO recipe the collapse comes from.

**Privileged rollout acquisition** leaves the update behind and asks where rollouts come from. On-policy RL and on-policy distillation can hold privileged information such as an answer or a verifier trace while still sampling as if blind. Pedagogical RL moves that decision upstream: a privileged self-teacher samples trajectories conditioned on $(x, c)$, and its reward is the product of task success and a student-scored learnability term. That changes the rollout contract, because a batch now carries two views of the same trajectory, one for the teacher and one for the student, and the student update becomes a gated imitation step where a scalar reward toggle used to sit. Carrying two policies and two prompt views through the scalar `rl_sandbox/` registry would mean threading them past every method that has no use for them, so Pedagogical RL gets its own package in `pedagogy_sandbox/`.

**Search coverage under vector rewards** is the last axis, and VPO sits here. It starts from a different deployment assumption: if test-time search will choose among many candidates, then post-training should not always collapse probability mass onto the single best scalarized answer. Once reward decomposes into components, the object worth optimizing is a candidate set covering several reward trade-offs, and that reaches back into the rollout contract itself. A VPO rollout contains multiple candidates, each candidate has a reward vector, and the group advantage comes from a set-level score computed over sampled scalarizations. None of that survives being expressed as a loss toggle on scalar rollouts, so VPO also gets its own package in `vpo_sandbox/`.

Each method or companion sandbox makes a bet on one or two of these axes. The repo is built so I can compare those bets on tasks small enough to inspect the gradient directly, which moves the discussion away from which method scored highest and toward which axis of disagreement a given comparison actually exercises.

## What is in here

- [`rl_sandbox/`](rl_sandbox/) is the toy sandbox itself, with bandit and sequence tasks and a registry of update rules covering PG, GRPO, DG, TPO, and the smaller families that each probe one axis at a time. Its [README](rl_sandbox/README.md) lays out the method-family menu, the task list, and the experiment matrix I work from when deciding what to run next.
- [`learnability_sandbox/`](learnability_sandbox/) is the sequential-environment companion, and the one that has grown furthest past its original scope. It derives the reward-contrast phase boundary in an analytical unique-path task, then calibrates that mechanism against the released puzzle topology from *Understanding Reasoning from Pretraining to Post-Training*. From there it builds a first-principles account of why RL curves are log-linear, why pass@k stagnates above the training group size, and which checkpoint coordinate should predict RL's payoff for a mid-trained model, with seven measured results on the released chess artifacts testing those claims. Its strict-termination and teacher-forced-reply environments expose a second boundary, between reward availability and causal credit, since reply replay preserves the terminal reward distribution exactly while adding policy tokens after the outcome is already fixed. Its [README](learnability_sandbox/README.md) is self-contained and carries the derivations, the evidence, and the next falsifiable experiments.
- [`opd_sandbox/`](opd_sandbox/) is the on-policy distillation appendix. It isolates estimator variance, support truncation, warmup schedules, and teacher-entropy effects on a smaller toy task where each design knob can be moved one at a time. Its [README](opd_sandbox/README.md) walks the five appendix experiments and what each one isolates.
- [`rlm_grpo/`](rlm_grpo/) is the one piece that scales beyond a toy: a standalone flow for training small causal LMs (0.5-0.6B) with GRPO under a recursive tree-of-rollouts sampling protocol. The design problem worth solving there is how the root reward should propagate through a tree whose shape the model itself decides at rollout time. Its [README](rlm_grpo/README.md) covers the credit-propagation rule and its rationale.
- [`vpo_sandbox/`](vpo_sandbox/) is the vector-reward companion. It keeps VPO separate because set-valued rollouts and reward vectors are a different batch contract, not a scalar loss toggle. Its [README](vpo_sandbox/README.md) covers the scoped VPO estimator, the scalar baselines, and the best@k and diversity metrics.
- [`pedagogy_sandbox/`](pedagogy_sandbox/) is the privileged rollout-acquisition companion. It keeps Pedagogical RL separate because the method has two policies, two prompt views, teacher-side learnability scoring by a frozen student, and student-side surprisal-gated imitation. Its [README](pedagogy_sandbox/README.md) covers the scoped implementation and baselines.
- [`rl_sandbox/analysis/`](rl_sandbox/analysis/) is the evidence: reproduction commands, result tables, and the figures the README embeds, all reproducible end to end on a single GPU.

```bash
pip install -e .

python -m rl_sandbox.train --task token_reversal --method TPO \
  --batch_size 96 --group_size 8 --inner_epochs 4 \
  --num_steps 300 --eval_every 20 --num_seeds 3

python -m vpo_sandbox.train --method VPOGRPO \
  --batch_size 128 --group_size 8 --num_candidates 3 \
  --num_steps 300 --eval_every 20 --num_seeds 3

python -m pedagogy_sandbox.train --method PedagogicalRL \
  --batch_size 96 --group_size 8 \
  --num_steps 300 --eval_every 20 --num_seeds 3

python -m opd_sandbox.train --task reversal --method OPDReverseKL \
  --batch_size 64 --num_steps 300 --eval_every 20

python -m learnability_sandbox.train \
  --horizon 3 --initial_correct_probability 0.5 \
  --group_size 8 --groups_per_step 64 \
  --num_steps 200 --num_seeds 3
```

## Inferences from these runs

Six inferences I take from the scalar sandbox methods after running them through compact three-seed sweeps. The VPO and pedagogy sandboxes are newer slices, documented as contracts first and not yet folded into these scalar result claims. The learnability sandbox carries its own seven measured results in its own README, since its evidence is about environments and not about update rules.

The runs below are short and the batches are small, so I treat the absolute numbers as regime checks. What survived enough variation to build mechanism on is the ordering between methods and the qualitative shape of each failure mode, both of which held steady across seed-to-seed variance and across small changes in horizon. Each headline states an inference and the mechanism I think drives it, and the section under it walks through the evidence.

The six are readings of one underlying map. Five land on the axes from the previous section, one apiece: TPO against GRPO on influence allocation, replay on support and coverage, token-level credit on credit granularity, reward-noise heuristics on reward uncertainty, and GRPO entropy collapse on optimization geometry. The sixth is the follow-up the third one forces. If token-level credit cannot manufacture signal where the reward function is silent, something else has to source that signal, and dense correction is the cheapest mechanism I could think to test.

1. **TPO extracts more from each rollout group than GRPO does.** On clean token-reversal, TPO finishes ahead of GRPO by more than the seed-to-seed variance, and the entropy diagnostics rule out faster collapse as the explanation. At fixed rollout cost, TPO produces K weighted gradient directions per group, one per candidate, where GRPO produces a single one, the sampled action's score function scaled by the standardized advantage. That difference in information per step is what surfaces as the test-error gap.
2. **Replay's useful band is set by effective sample age, not buffer capacity.** Replay is a two-knob trade, with capacity controlling variance and age controlling bias. Report capacity alone and you have tuned one knob while leaving the other wherever your particular setup happened to put it, which is why conclusions reached that way travel so badly to a setup that sets it differently.
3. **Token-level credit redistributes existing reward signal but cannot create it.** Anywhere the reward function is silent the advantage is zero, so the per-token gradient contribution vanishes however careful the upstream credit-assignment scheme was. The trap is reaching for finer credit assignment when the bottleneck is reward sparsity. Routing signal and sourcing signal are different jobs, and only reward shaping does the second one.
4. **Reward-noise heuristics are operating points on a true-versus-false-positive trade-off, not free improvements.** Every method that downweights the update when the reward looks uncertain is filtering some true signal away as collateral and accepting some false signal as truth. That makes calibration at a stated noise rate the thing to compare, since robustness in the abstract has no operating point attached to it.
5. **GRPO's entropy collapse lives in the standardization, upstream of the clip.** Within-group standardization scales $(R_i - \mu)/\sigma$ as $1/\sigma$ when the rollouts in a group mostly agree, which is the common case on an easy task. The PPO clip sits downstream of that amplification, so it bounds the magnitude of each step while leaving untouched the direction the step is being pushed in. The variants that have actually slowed entropy decay in the literature, DrGRPO and DAPO, both go after the normalization, and neither one tightens the clip.
6. **An oracle reviser does source the signal that token-level credit cannot, but the result belongs to the oracle until proven otherwise.** On chain-reversal, both reviser-based methods reach zero exact-match error before supervised CE does, with SelfDistillDG arriving around step 467 against CE's 733. That is the shaping answer to inference 3, and it is also the one result here I would not yet transfer, because the reviser is the task's own labels. Whether dense correction becomes a usable lever turns on whether the speedup survives a learned, noisier reviser.

### TPO extracts more from each rollout group than GRPO does

The size of the gap on clean token-reversal made me look closer. Final test error sits at 0.24 for TPO against 0.35 for GRPO, with seed spreads of 0.06 and 0.01, so the gap clears the seed-to-seed variance comfortably. The most charitable alternative explanation is that TPO wins by collapsing exploration faster and settling on a sharper policy, and the entropy diagnostics rule that out. TPO keeps final entropy near DG's and never crosses the 0.1 threshold within 300 steps, while GRPO crosses it around step 53. Whatever TPO is doing, it is not paying for its lead in exploration.

The lead comes from using more of each rollout group. With `group_size=8`, GRPO reads the group as a noise-reduction device. It standardizes within the group, computes a clean advantage for the one action that was actually sampled, and applies that advantage to the score function of that action. The other seven rollouts shaped the standardization and then went unused for direction. TPO reads those same eight rollouts as candidate next-actions, builds a soft target distribution over them weighted by relative rewards, and pulls the policy toward that target. That leaves GRPO with one weighted gradient direction per group and TPO with K of them, one per candidate, at negligible extra compute on rollouts that have already been paid for. At fixed rollout cost the K-direction construction uses more of what those rollouts measured, and the extra information per step shows up as a faster decrease in test error.

Whether the gap survives at scale is genuinely open, and the mechanism cuts both ways, which is what makes this an experiment to run instead of an assumption to carry forward. It is the first of the two below.

### Replay's useful band is set by sample age, not buffer capacity

The replay sweep nearly fooled me. The table reads simply: capacity 5 at delay 4 is fine, with test error between 0.49 and 0.50 and no entropy collapse, while capacity 32 at the same delay is a disaster, with test error at 1.00 for vanilla ReplayDG and full entropy collapse. "Small buffers fine, big buffers not" fits those numbers, and it is close to what I wrote down before checking. The trouble is that capacity is not the mechanism, so the next replay-augmented method to come along will be tuned differently enough that the summary breaks on contact with it.

Replay is a variance-versus-bias trade with two separate knobs. Recycling samples reduces the variance of the gradient, and the reduction scales with the buffer's effective sample count. Meanwhile every recycled sample comes from a policy that has since moved, and the bias each one contributes scales with how stale it has become. The two knobs are coupled, because age is itself a function of capacity, freshness decay, and how fast the policy is moving. Report capacity alone, the way most replay comparisons do, and you have drawn a single-axis tuning curve through a two-axis trade-off.

The capacity-5-at-delay-4 regime turns out to be the boring case for a freshness-aware method. Every sample in the buffer is exactly 4 steps old by construction, leaving no spread for the method to read. FreshDG is only marginally more stable than delayed DG in those rows for that reason, since its age weights have nothing to work with. The capacity-32 rows decouple capacity from age and let the age axis swing, and that is the stress test. Mean sample age climbs to roughly 16 to 18 steps, well past the nominal delay, and the gradient ends up averaging over policies the current one no longer resembles. Without freshness decay that averaged direction is wrong often enough to collapse entropy and drive error to chance. With `--replay_age_decay 0.5` the effective sample age halves to around 7.4, the bias drops back into the range importance ratios can absorb, and final error recovers to roughly delayed-DG.

So the variable to report is the effective age distribution the buffer produces, read together with the freshness-decay schedule that shaped it, and that distribution belongs alongside the final-error number in any future replay comparison. Capacity on its own leaves the reader unable to tell whether replay bought anything.

### Token-level credit redistributes signal but cannot create it

The masked-reversal experiment was instrumented to ask whether token-level credit improves learning at scored positions without damaging unscored ones. The answer came back half encouraging and half cautionary, and only the cautionary half changed how I choose methods afterward. TPOToken drives the scored suffix to zero error within 300 steps, which is about the cleanest per-position routing result anyone could hope for. The credit lands where the reward function placed signal, and the method makes no pretence of learning at the positions the reward function declined to score. Unscored positions, meanwhile, sit near chance throughout. That residual error is not a failure waiting to be fixed. It is a faithful reflection of what the reward function asked the optimizer to do.

The token-level policy gradient shows why. Any per-token update has the form $\nabla_\theta \log \pi(a_t \mid s_t)\, A_t$. Wherever the reward function places no signal, $A_t$ is zero, and zero advantage multiplies the score function back to zero however clever the upstream credit-assignment scheme was. Token-level credit *redistributes* whatever reward signal already exists, concentrating it where the reward function placed it. Sourcing new signal is a different operation, and no arrangement of the routing performs it.

That distinction is easy to lose, and losing it costs a run. Read the masked-reversal scored-suffix accuracy on its own and token-level credit looks like it works, which invites reaching for it on a task whose reward is sparse rather than partially located. On such a task the bottleneck is whether the reward function carries usable signal anywhere, so no amount of care about allocating that signal across positions helps, and reward shaping is the tool that does. The unscored column of the masked-reversal table separates the two cases cleanly, which is why I would lead with it in any future comparison of token-level methods.

### Reward-noise heuristics are operating points, not free improvements

The reward-noise sweep is what made me give up the phrase "noise-robust." Every method in it downweights or drops updates when some uncertainty proxy fires, and they are all making the same structural choice: an operating point on the trade between filtering out true reward signal as collateral and accepting false reward signal as truth. At a fixed noise rate, 0.2 in this sweep, no proxy operating without ground truth can avoid paying on one side of that trade.

FilteredDG showed most clearly how much the proxy itself can confound the comparison. In the ungrouped runs used here its uncertainty signal is computed at the batch level, so the threshold sees one value across the whole batch and acts as an all-or-nothing switch where per-sample filtering was intended. At its default threshold of 0.5 it accepts essentially every batch and reproduces the no-filter baseline. Drop the threshold to 0.2 and it rejects essentially every batch and learns nothing, with test error climbing from 0.38 to 0.65 and variance widening to match, which is what an optimizer with almost no gradient signal should look like. Threshold tuning is not what turned the method into a single-bit gate. The granularity of the proxy did, which says something about this implementation of the uncertainty signal and nothing about filtering as an idea.

ASPO and R2VPO hold up better under false-positive rare-token noise, finishing within a hair of DG's test error, and they pay for it in exploration, ending at entropy 0.15 and 0.18 against DG's 0.96. The conservative gates UncertaintyDG and RewardVarianceDG take the opposite trade, holding entropy near DG's level and paying in learning speed. The entropy column is where that cost becomes visible at all, since the test-error column shows only what each method bought.

Calibration, then, is the unit that survives this sweep, in place of robustness. At a given noise rate, how much true signal is a method willing to sacrifice to keep out how much false signal? Each method answers differently, which puts them at different points on one curve, and a fair comparison plots them there.

### GRPO's entropy collapse lives in the standardization, upstream of the clip

Of all the diagnostics in the sandbox, the entropy sweep on GRPO is the one that moved my thinking furthest. I started where the PPO heritage points: GRPO collapses entropy because the clipped surrogate lets the policy ratio swing too far per step, so tighten the clip. The data sends that story back. DGEntropyGuard acts at the sampled-action probability level, downstream of the same standardization, and it barely moves the entropy needle. The variants that have actually slowed GRPO's entropy decay in the literature, DrGRPO and DAPO, get their traction upstream, at the standardization.

The mechanism lives in the advantage formula. GRPO standardizes rewards within each rollout group, computing $A_i = (R_i - \mu)/\sigma$. Standardization is a sensible noise-reduction step while $\sigma$ stays on the same order as the true reward spread, and on an easy task it does not. Most rollouts in a group agree on the right answer, $\sigma$ shrinks toward zero, and the standardized advantage scales as $1/\sigma$ however small the raw reward gap $R_i - \mu$ happened to be. A gap of 0.01 between two near-identical rollouts produces a standardized advantage of order 1 when $\sigma$ is also around 0.01, and nothing in $A_i$ tells the optimizer that the underlying signal was tiny. Standardization is doing exactly what it was designed to do, so the amplification is structural. What I think the literature on the GRPO entropy problem has partly missed is how that design interacts with PPO-style clipping.

The PPO clip bounds the policy ratio to the interval $[1-\epsilon,\ 1+\epsilon]$ per step, which bounds how far one update can move the policy. It leaves the advantage driving that update untouched. At the clip boundary the clipped surrogate's gradient is still proportional to $A_i$, the very quantity the standardization has just amplified. Every update therefore saturates the trust region in the direction of whichever rollout happened to win its group, and the saturation continues for as many steps as it takes for entropy to decay below useful levels. The sweep puts that crossing of 0.1 at step 53, with a seed spread of 12, a much tighter horizon than the loss curve suggests. Once the entropy is gone the policy stops exploring and the test-error curve flattens whatever the clip is doing.

Where the fix lives decides which interventions are worth running, so this is more than a diagnosis. The clip sits downstream of the standardization, so tightening it constrains the magnitude of each step while leaving alone both the direction the step is pushed in and the amplification that put it there. DGEntropyGuard sits downstream too, at the sampled-action probability level, which is consistent with how little it helps. Any fix has to act above the clip. DrGRPO does that by dropping the reward-std normalization in favor of group-centering alone, cutting the $\sigma$-driven amplification at its source. DAPO decouples the clipping range so positive and negative updates use different bounds, going after the asymmetry instead of the standardization. Both reach the part of the recipe that generates the broken signal in the first place, and if the toy mechanism is right, both should show their advantage by slowing the entropy decay measured here before any test-error gap opens up.

### An oracle reviser sources signal, but the oracle is doing the work

The previous section ends on a negative result. Token-level credit cannot create gradient where the reward function is silent, which leaves reward shaping as the tool for a sparse-reward task. Dense correction is the cheapest version of that shaping I could build, and chain-reversal is the task built to test it, since its reward chain places signal only at trajectory level while the underlying task has obvious per-token structure.

My first attempt at the comparison was under-budget, and I record it because the failure wore the costume of a method result. At 300 steps, exact-match error on chain-reversal discriminates between nothing at all. Supervised CE itself finishes near 0.99 error, so no method has room to show a speedup over a baseline that has not finished either. Extending to 1500 steps makes the question answerable, and the ordering inverts relative to what the short run had suggested. SelfDistillDG first reaches zero error around step 467 and SCOPELite around 533, both well ahead of CE at 733. On this task, converting sparse trajectory reward into dense token supervision is worth roughly 200 to 300 steps against a supervised oracle.

I hold this one at arm's length because the reviser here is the task's own labels. That makes the experiment a clean test of whether the mechanism can work at all, and no test whatsoever of whether it works when the reviser is fallible. Every interesting deployment of the idea swaps the oracle for a learned reviser or a process reward model, which walks straight into the teacher-quality confound the `opd_sandbox` appendix exists to study, and a noisy reviser could plausibly spend its whole correction budget reinforcing its own errors. What I carry forward is therefore the shape of the question and not the 200-step number. Dense correction becomes a real lever only if the speedup degrades gracefully as reviser accuracy falls, and measuring that curve is the experiment I have not run.

## Predictions worth testing at scale

The toy runs make two predictions I would want to put to larger experiments. In each case the toy has identified a mechanism, and what the larger run tests is how far that mechanism travels, so both outcomes teach something.

The first is whether TPO's information-per-step advantage holds up at scale. The gap exists in the toy because grouped rollouts contain more candidate-target information than GRPO's normalization step extracts, and scale pushes on that from two directions at once. A stronger base policy concentrates its action distribution near the right answer, so the single sampled action already carries most of the signal TPO is currently squeezing out of K candidates, and the gap should close. Richer reward signals, meaning per-token rewards, multi-criterion judges, or per-segment scoring, put more structure into TPO's per-candidate weights, and the gap should widen. Which force dominates is what a same-task TPO-against-GRPO comparison with a 1B-class base under fixed rollout cost would settle, since it reduces the question to whether the K-candidate extraction still earns its compute once the base policy is strong. That is where I would put the next budget.

The second is where the entropy-collapse fix for the GRPO family lives. The toy mechanism puts the standardization at the load-bearing position in the entropy decay, with the clip sitting downstream of it, and an ablation tests that directly. Hold the PPO clip fixed at its standard band, drop only the reward-std normalization to recover the DrGRPO arm, and measure whether the entropy decay slows. If the mechanism transfers, that slowdown should survive across base-model sizes. The control is tightening the clip while leaving the normalization alone, which on this account should barely move the entropy decay, since the clip is downstream of where the broken signal gets generated. Should both ablations slow entropy comparably at scale, the toy diagnosis was wrong and the clip carries more of the work than the small-scale mechanism suggests. That outcome is worth having too, because it pins the toy-to-scale disagreement to one identified step in the recipe instead of leaving it spread across the whole thing.

## Scope

The sandboxes run on a single machine, with CUDA when a GPU is available and CPU otherwise. The methods themselves are scoped to their local batch and task contracts. From each published method I keep the part that decides the update, meaning the update rule, the credit-assignment scheme, and the normalization choices, and drop the distributed-system scaffolding a toy setup has no use for. Some scoping decisions would change what a method means and not merely how large it runs, for instance running advantage normalization in a regime where the original paper assumes a critic. In those cases the trainer rejects the config, since an arm whose name promises one estimator and whose output reflects another will quietly corrupt every comparison it appears in.

The companions follow the same rule. VPO's local implementation is faithful to the set-reward estimator and explicit that it does not reproduce the paper's large LM domains. Pedagogical RL keeps privileged teacher GRPO, product-form spike-aware rewards, and surprisal-gated assimilation, and makes no claim on the paper's large-model domains. The OPD appendix uses an oracle teacher, which keeps its design-choice questions clear of teacher-quality confounds. The learnability sandbox is the one that reaches outside, since its chess results are computed against released checkpoints and datasets pinned at fixed revisions, which its README documents.

Distributed rollout, learned critics, and production reward pipelines all stay out of scope. Each one would cost the observability that the small scale exists to buy, and observability is the whole return on working this small.
