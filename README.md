# rl-experiments

Six connected pieces of code for studying what RL update rules actually do to a policy. The base is [`rl_sandbox/`](rl_sandbox/), a small PyTorch sandbox of bandit and sequence tasks with a registry of update rules to compare on them. Next to it sit five companions, each one built because some method broke the assumptions the scalar sandbox is built on. [`learnability_sandbox/`](learnability_sandbox/) works one step upstream of every update rule, at the boundary where a policy, an environment, and a rollout budget either produce usable reward contrast or leave the optimizer with no task direction at all. [`rlm_grpo/`](rlm_grpo/) trains small causal LMs (0.5-0.6B) with GRPO on a tree-of-rollouts protocol. [`opd_sandbox/`](opd_sandbox/) is an appendix probing on-policy distillation mechanics on similar toy tasks. [`vpo_sandbox/`](vpo_sandbox/) isolates Vector Policy Optimization on a set-valued, vector-reward toy, where the question is whether training should preserve a pool of trade-offs for test-time search instead of collapsing onto one answer. [`pedagogy_sandbox/`](pedagogy_sandbox/) isolates Pedagogical RL as a privileged rollout-acquisition protocol with separate teacher and student policies.

The question driving all six is older than any particular objective family. When the reward signal is sparse, noisy, delayed, privileged, or vector-valued, which rollouts deserve gradient credit, how should they be found, and at what granularity should a policy spend its update budget? Benchmarks rank methods by who finished first. They cannot tell you why one method got there and another stalled, or whether a winner at small scale will still win at a larger one. I wanted to watch the training step itself: where gradient mass concentrates as the policy moves, when the importance ratio drifts past the band the clipped surrogate tracks, at what step entropy collapses past recovery, when privileged information produces learnable rather than merely correct rollouts, and when a policy has quietly collapsed a useful candidate pool into a single scalar compromise. These signals are the first casualty of scale, because distributed rollouts and large batches average them out, and the failure only surfaces after days of compute have already gone into it. A single GPU with toy models and short horizons brings the signals back into view, and the rest of this setup exists to keep them readable.

## How I think about the space of choices

Post-training RL papers usually organize themselves by objective family: PPO, GRPO, DPO, RLVR, rejection sampling, distillation. After spending enough time reading them, that framing started to feel too coarse for the decisions I actually had to make. Two methods inside the same family can disagree about the choice that determines the run, and two methods from very different lineages can agree on it. The framing that has been more useful to me sits a level below the objective.

> Given a fixed budget of online rollouts, learner updates, and KL distance from the reference policy, which samples should receive gradient weight, at what granularity, and for what downstream use of the candidate pool?

The question splits into seven axes, and the order matters. The first two ask how to use the reward signal you already collected. The third asks when that signal is still relevant to the current policy. The fourth asks when the reward function itself is trustworthy. The fifth asks how the optimizer responds to the choices upstream of it. The sixth asks how privileged information should help find rollouts before the student can find them on-policy. The seventh steps back and asks whether training should produce one answer at all, or a searchable set of alternatives. Each axis is also a failure mode I can reproduce cheaply, which is the whole reason I can stare at the gradient when something goes wrong.

**Influence allocation** is the first. Every rollout arrives with an advantage, a surprisal, and a sampled action, and how those three get turned into a per-sample weight is a real design choice rather than a bookkeeping detail. Uniform weighting anchors the axis as the no-effect baseline and advantage-only weighting is the standard policy-gradient choice. The delight family (DG and Kondo, from Delightful Policy Gradient, [arXiv:2603.14608](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.14608)) is the third bet, and it is worth being precise about what it does, because the shorthand "advantage times surprisal weighting" gets the mechanism wrong. The product `advantage * surprisal` is not the weight. It is the argument to a sigmoid gate, and the gate then multiplies the advantage, so the effective weight is `sigmoid(advantage * surprisal / eta) * advantage`. Since surprisal is never negative, the product carries the sign of the advantage, and the gate does something asymmetric with it: a surprising success saturates the gate toward one and gets full credit, a surprising failure drives it toward zero and gets suppressed, and both unsurprising cases sit near one half. The bet is therefore not that surprise deserves more weight in general, but that a policy should learn hard from lucky wins and discount unlucky losses.

GRPO sits on the same axis despite the different vocabulary, because its group-normalized advantage is just another choice of weighting. The one method that escapes the axis entirely is TPO, which does not reweight a sample-driven update at all. It changes what the update target is, treating the rollouts in a group as candidates for a soft target distribution rather than as one sampled action and seven noise reducers. The right comparison for TPO, in this framing, is not whether its weighting beats GRPO's, but whether its candidate-target construction extracts more from the same grouped rollouts than GRPO's normalization step does.

**Credit granularity** is the second. Even with the weighting scheme fixed, an update still has to attribute the reward somewhere, either to the whole trajectory, to a branch within a tree of rollouts, or to individual tokens. Sparse-reward tasks need a credit story even when the feedback is a single bit, because flattening one bit across an entire trajectory drowns the meaningful tokens in noise from the unmeaningful ones. Dense-reward tasks have the opposite problem: collapsing positions that carry per-step signal back into a single sequence-level score throws away most of what the reward function was telling you. TPOToken and DGToken are the per-token bets in this sandbox, and `masked_reversal` is the task built to expose their trade-offs, because it gives the comparison both scored and unscored positions to read.

**Support and coverage** is the third, and it is really the question of when logged data is still informative for the current policy. Replay reduces gradient variance by recycling samples across optimizer steps, but every recycled sample comes from a policy that has since moved, and the importance ratios meant to correct for that mismatch are themselves noisy. A rollout collected k steps ago is approximately on-policy only while the policy has not moved much in k steps, so the useful capacity of any replay buffer is whatever fits inside that freshness window. Outside the window, the gradient points at a policy that has already moved past where it was collected. ReplayDG and FreshDG are the diagnostics on this axis, and what the runs say is that the controlling variable is the effective age distribution of the buffer rather than its capacity.

**Reward uncertainty** is the fourth, and it becomes load-bearing whenever the reward is a proxy, such as a verifier, a judge, or a process reward model. Any proxy makes mistakes: some fraction of its high signals are false positives, and some fraction of its low signals are missed credit. What makes this axis clean to study in the sandbox is that three of its methods share a single uncertainty signal, the within-group reward standard deviation, and differ only in where they inject it. UncertaintyDG subtracts it inside the gate argument, which shifts the gate's threshold. RewardVarianceDG divides the advantage by it, shrinking the magnitude before the gate ever sees it. FilteredDG applies a hard keep-or-drop mask outside the gate. Same signal, three insertion points, three different failure modes, which is a cleaner comparison than three methods that also disagree about what uncertainty means.

ASPO and R2VPO belong to a different question on the same axis, and the sandbox keeps them here because they answer it in opposite ways. ASPO ([arXiv:2510.06062](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.06062)) inverts the importance ratio for positive-advantage tokens, so a rare good action gets more gradient rather than less, and it clips one-sidedly to keep that from running away. R2VPO ([arXiv:2601.03320](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03320)) drops hard clipping altogether in favor of a penalty on ratio variance, which preserves the gradient of a rare high-advantage sample instead of zeroing it at a clip boundary. So DG and ASPO both amplify rare breakthroughs while R2VPO merely declines to destroy them, and the comparison asks whether amplification is necessary or preservation is already enough.

They fail differently from each other, but they share a structural property the noise sweep makes visible. Every method that downweights the update when the reward looks uncertain is choosing an operating point on the same true-versus-false-positive trade-off. There is no free filtering on this axis, only different points on the curve.

**Optimization geometry** is the fifth, meaning clipping, ratio variance, normalization, KL terms, entropy penalties, and the way all of these interact. The wrong combination can collapse entropy inside a hundred steps while the loss curve still looks healthy, and a healthy-looking loss curve is exactly what makes this failure so expensive to find at scale. The entropy-collapse sweep is the cheapest setting in which to watch it happen, and it points clearly at where in the GRPO recipe the collapse is coming from.

**Privileged rollout acquisition** is the sixth. On-policy RL and on-policy distillation can hold privileged information such as an answer or a verifier trace while still sampling as if blind. Pedagogical RL moves that decision upstream: a privileged self-teacher samples trajectories conditioned on `(x, c)`, but its reward is the product of task success and a student-scored learnability term. That changes the rollout contract, because a batch now carries two views of the same trajectory, one for the teacher and one for the student, and the student update becomes a gated imitation step rather than a scalar reward toggle. That is why Pedagogical RL lives in `pedagogy_sandbox/` rather than the scalar `rl_sandbox/` registry.

**Search coverage under vector rewards** is the seventh, and VPO sits here. It starts from a different deployment assumption: if test-time search will choose among many candidates, then post-training should not always collapse probability mass onto the single best scalarized answer. When reward decomposes into components, the useful object is a candidate set that covers several reward trade-offs, and that changes the rollout contract itself. A VPO rollout contains multiple candidates, each candidate has a reward vector, and the group advantage comes from a set-level score computed over sampled scalarizations. VPO lives in `vpo_sandbox/` rather than the scalar registry because that change of contract is not a loss toggle.

Each method or companion sandbox makes a bet on one or two of these axes. The repo is built so I can compare those bets on tasks small enough to inspect the gradient directly, which shifts the methodological discussion away from which method scored highest and toward which axis of disagreement a given comparison actually exercises.

## What is in here

- [`rl_sandbox/`](rl_sandbox/) is the toy sandbox itself, with bandit and sequence tasks and a registry of update rules covering PG, GRPO, DG, TPO, and the smaller families that each probe one axis at a time. Its [README](rl_sandbox/README.md) lays out the method-family menu, the task list, and the experiment matrix I work from when deciding what to run next.
- [`learnability_sandbox/`](learnability_sandbox/) is the sequential-environment companion, and the one that has grown furthest past its original scope. It derives the reward-contrast phase boundary in an analytical unique-path task, then calibrates that mechanism against the released puzzle topology from *Understanding Reasoning from Pretraining to Post-Training*. From there it builds a first-principles account of why RL curves are log-linear, why pass@k stagnates above the training group size, and which checkpoint coordinate should predict RL's payoff for a mid-trained model, with seven measured results on the released chess artifacts testing those claims. Its strict-termination and teacher-forced-reply environments expose a second boundary, between reward availability and causal credit, since reply replay preserves the terminal reward distribution exactly while adding policy tokens after the outcome is already fixed. Its [README](learnability_sandbox/README.md) is self-contained and carries the derivations, the evidence, and the next falsifiable experiments.
- [`opd_sandbox/`](opd_sandbox/) is the on-policy distillation appendix. It isolates estimator variance, support truncation, warmup schedules, and teacher-entropy effects on a smaller toy task where each design knob can be moved one at a time. Its [README](opd_sandbox/README.md) walks the five appendix experiments and what each one isolates.
- [`rlm_grpo/`](rlm_grpo/) is the one piece that scales beyond a toy: a standalone flow for training small causal LMs (0.5-0.6B) with GRPO under a recursive tree-of-rollouts sampling protocol. The design problem worth solving there is how the root reward should propagate through a tree whose shape the model itself decides at rollout time. Its [README](rlm_grpo/README.md) covers the credit-propagation rule and its rationale.
- [`vpo_sandbox/`](vpo_sandbox/) is the vector-reward companion. It keeps VPO separate because set-valued rollouts and reward vectors are a different batch contract, not a scalar loss toggle. Its [README](vpo_sandbox/README.md) covers the scoped VPO estimator, the scalar baselines, and the best@k and diversity metrics.
- [`pedagogy_sandbox/`](pedagogy_sandbox/) is the privileged rollout-acquisition companion. It keeps Pedagogical RL separate because the method has two policies, two prompt views, teacher-side learnability scoring by a frozen student, and student-side surprisal-gated imitation. Its [README](pedagogy_sandbox/README.md) covers the scoped implementation and baselines.
- [`rl_sandbox/analysis/`](rl_sandbox/analysis/) is the evidence: reproduction commands, result tables, and the figures the README embeds, all reproducible end to end on a single GPU.

  --batch_size 96 --group_size 8 --inner_epochs 4 \
  --num_steps 300 --eval_every 20 --num_seeds 3

  --batch_size 128 --group_size 8 --num_candidates 3 \

## Inferences from these runs

Six inferences I take from the scalar sandbox methods after running them through compact three-seed sweeps. The VPO and pedagogy sandboxes are newer slices, documented as contracts first and not yet folded into these scalar result claims. The learnability sandbox carries its own seven measured results in its own README, since its evidence is about environments rather than update rules.

The runs below are short and the batches are small, so I read the absolute numbers as regime checks rather than benchmark claims. What I trust enough to draw mechanism from is the part that held steady: the orderings between methods and the qualitative shape of each failure mode were stable across seed-to-seed variance and across small changes in horizon. Each headline below states an inference and the mechanism I think drives it, and the section that follows walks through the evidence.

These are readings of the same underlying map rather than independent findings. Five of them land on the axes from the previous section one apiece, with TPO versus GRPO on influence allocation, replay on support and coverage, token-level credit on credit granularity, reward-noise heuristics on reward uncertainty, and GRPO entropy collapse on optimization geometry. The sixth is the follow-up the third one forces: if token-level credit cannot manufacture signal where the reward function is silent, then something has to source that signal instead, and dense correction is the cheapest mechanism worth testing.

1. **TPO extracts more from each rollout group than GRPO does.** On clean token-reversal, TPO finishes ahead of GRPO by more than the seed-to-seed variance, and the entropy diagnostics rule out faster collapse as the explanation. At fixed rollout cost, TPO produces K weighted gradient directions per group, one per candidate, while GRPO produces one, the sampled action's scaled by the standardized advantage. The information-per-step difference is what shows up as the test-error gap.
2. **Replay's useful band is set by effective sample age, not buffer capacity.** Capacity is the variance knob in the replay trade-off and age is the bias knob. A replay comparison that reports capacity without the age distribution it produces is tuning along one axis of a two-axis trade-off, and its conclusions transfer poorly to setups where the second knob takes a different value.
3. **Token-level credit redistributes existing reward signal but cannot create it.** Anywhere the reward function is silent the advantage is zero, so the per-token gradient contribution vanishes no matter how careful the upstream credit-assignment scheme is. The trap is reaching for finer credit assignment when the bottleneck is reward sparsity, where the right tool is reward shaping rather than routing.
4. **Reward-noise heuristics are operating points on a true-versus-false-positive trade-off, not free improvements.** Every method that downweights the update when the reward looks uncertain is filtering some true signal as collateral and accepting some false signal as truth. The framing for comparing them is calibration at a specific noise rate, not robustness in the abstract.
5. **GRPO's entropy collapse lives in the standardization, upstream of the clip.** Within-group standardization scales `(R_i - mu) / sigma` as `1/sigma` when the rollouts in a group mostly agree, which is the common case on an easy task. The PPO clip sits downstream of that amplification, so it bounds the magnitude of each step without constraining the direction the step is being pushed in. The variants that have actually slowed entropy decay in the literature, DrGRPO and DAPO, attack the normalization rather than tightening the clip.
6. **An oracle reviser does source the signal that token-level credit cannot, but the result belongs to the oracle until proven otherwise.** On chain-reversal, both reviser-based methods reach zero exact-match error before supervised CE does, with SelfDistillDG arriving around step 467 against CE's 733. That is the shaping answer to inference 3, and it is also the one result here I would not yet transfer, because the reviser is the task's own labels. The question that decides whether dense correction is a usable lever is whether the speedup survives a learned, noisier reviser.

### TPO extracts more from each rollout group than GRPO does

The size of the gap on clean token-reversal made me look closer. Final test error sits at 0.24 +/- 0.06 for TPO and 0.35 +/- 0.01 for GRPO, comfortably more than the seed-to-seed variance. Before reading anything into that, I checked the most charitable alternative explanation: perhaps TPO wins by collapsing exploration faster and converging on a sharper policy. The entropy diagnostics rule it out. TPO keeps final entropy near DG's and never crosses the 0.1 threshold within 300 steps, while GRPO crosses it around step 53. Whatever TPO is doing, it is not buying its lead with reduced exploration.

The lead comes from using more of each rollout group. With `group_size=8`, GRPO reads the group as a noise-reduction device: it standardizes within the group, computes a clean advantage for the one action that was actually sampled, and applies that advantage to the score function of that action. The other seven rollouts contributed to the standardization but went unused for direction. TPO treats the same eight rollouts differently, reading them as candidate next-actions, building a soft target distribution over them weighted by relative rewards, and pulling the policy toward that soft target. GRPO therefore produces one weighted gradient direction per group and TPO produces K of them, one per candidate, at negligible extra compute on rollouts that have already been paid for. At fixed rollout cost the K-direction construction uses more of what the rollouts measured, and the information-per-step difference shows up as a faster decrease in test error.

Whether the gap survives at scale is genuinely open, and the mechanism cuts both ways, which is what makes it worth testing rather than assuming. That experiment is the first of the two below.

### Replay's useful band is set by sample age, not buffer capacity

The replay sweep nearly fooled me. The table reads simply: capacity 5 at delay 4 is fine (test error 0.49 to 0.50, no entropy collapse), and capacity 32 at the same delay is a disaster (test error 1.00 for vanilla ReplayDG, full entropy collapse). "Small buffers fine, big buffers not" fits those numbers, and it is what I almost wrote down. The trouble is that it is not the mechanism, so the next replay-augmented method to come along will be tuned differently enough that the wrong takeaway transfers badly.

Replay is really a variance-versus-bias trade with two separate knobs. On the variance side, recycling samples reduces the variance of the gradient, and the reduction scales with the buffer's effective sample count. On the bias side, every recycled sample comes from a policy that has since moved, and the bias each sample contributes scales with how stale it has become. The two knobs are not independent, because age is itself a function of capacity, freshness decay, and the speed at which the policy is moving. Reporting only capacity, the way most replay comparisons do, gives a single-axis tuning curve for a two-axis trade-off.

The capacity-5-at-delay-4 regime turns out to be the boring case for a freshness-aware method, because by construction every sample in the buffer is exactly 4 steps old and there is no spread to compare across. FreshDG is only marginally more stable than delayed DG in those rows for exactly that reason: its age weights have nothing meaningful to work with. The capacity-32 rows are the stress test that decouples capacity from age and lets the age axis swing. Mean sample age climbs to roughly 16 to 18 steps, well past the nominal delay, and the gradient ends up averaging over policies the current one no longer resembles. Without freshness decay the averaged direction is wrong often enough to collapse entropy and drive error to chance. With `--replay_age_decay 0.5`, the effective sample age halves to around 7.4, the bias drops back into the range importance ratios can absorb, and final error recovers to roughly delayed-DG.

The implication for any future replay comparison is that buffer capacity by itself is the wrong variable to report. What decides whether replay is buying or costing is the effective age distribution the buffer produces in combination with the freshness-decay schedule, and that distribution is what belongs alongside the final-error number. Comparisons that report only capacity are doing the right experiment on the wrong axis.

### Token-level credit redistributes signal but cannot create it

The masked-reversal experiment was instrumented to ask whether token-level credit improves learning at scored positions without damaging unscored ones. The answer came back half encouraging and half cautionary, and only the cautionary half changes my future choices. On the encouraging side, TPOToken drives the scored suffix to zero error within 300 steps, which is about the cleanest per-position routing result anyone could hope for: the credit lands where the reward function placed signal, and the method does not pretend to learn at the positions the reward function declined to score. On the cautionary side, unscored positions sit near chance throughout, and that residual error is not a fixable failure mode. It is a faithful reflection of what the reward function asked the optimizer to do.

The token-level policy gradient itself shows why. Any per-token update has the form `grad_theta log pi(a_t | s_t) * A_t`. At any position where the reward function places no signal, `A_t` is zero, and zero advantage multiplies the score function back to zero no matter how clever the upstream credit-assignment scheme was. No token-level method can manufacture gradient at positions the reward function declined to score. Token-level credit *redistributes* whatever reward signal already exists across positions, concentrating it where the reward function placed it, and it does not *source* new signal. Keeping that distinction between routing reward and sourcing reward in view is what tells you which tool to reach for.

Here is the trap an outside reader is most likely to walk into. Look at the masked-reversal scored-suffix accuracy in isolation and it is tempting to conclude that token-level credit works, then reach for it on a different task where the reward signal is sparse rather than partially located. That conclusion picks the wrong tool, because a sparse-reward task needs reward shaping rather than finer credit assignment. The bottleneck there is whether the reward function carries usable signal at all, not how the signal gets allocated across positions. The unscored column of the masked-reversal table exposes the distinction cleanly, and I would put it first in any future comparison of token-level methods.

### Reward-noise heuristics are operating points, not free improvements

After the reward-noise sweep I stopped using the phrase "noise-robust" and started saying something more honest. Every method in the sweep downweights or drops updates when some uncertainty proxy fires, and they are all making the same structural choice: an operating point on the trade-off between filtering out true reward signal as collateral and accepting false reward signal as truth. At a fixed noise rate, 0.2 in this sweep, no proxy that operates without ground truth can avoid paying on one side of that trade.

FilteredDG was the clearest demonstration of how much the proxy can confound the comparison. With its default threshold of 0.5 and ungrouped runs, its uncertainty signal is computed at the batch level, so the threshold sees the same value across the whole batch and ends up acting as all-or-nothing thresholding rather than per-sample filtering. At threshold 0.5 it accepts essentially every batch and reproduces the no-filter baseline. Drop the threshold to 0.2 and it rejects essentially every batch and learns nothing, with test error climbing from 0.38 to 0.65 and much wider variance, which is what one would expect from an optimizer that has almost no gradient signal. The proxy's granularity, not the threshold tuning, made the method behave as a single-bit gate. The failure was the proxy rather than filtering as such.

ASPO and R2VPO are sturdier under false-positive rare-token noise, with final test error within a hair of DG's, but they pay for it with very low final entropy, 0.15 and 0.18 against DG's 0.96. The conservative gates UncertaintyDG and RewardVarianceDG preserve entropy near DG's level and pay in slower learning instead. Reading the entropy column alongside the test-error column reveals the trade-off that the test-error column alone hides: every robustness gain in this sweep is being paid for in either exploration or speed, and any comparison that reports the gain without the cost has reported half the operating point.

So "noise robustness" turns out to be the wrong unit on this task. The right unit is calibration. At a given noise rate, how much true signal are you willing to sacrifice to keep out how much false signal? Different methods sit at different points on that curve, and a fair comparison plots them on it rather than picking a winner in the abstract.

### GRPO's entropy collapse lives in the standardization, upstream of the clip

Of all the diagnostics in the sandbox, the entropy sweep on GRPO is the one that has shifted my thinking the most. I started with the framing the PPO heritage encourages, where GRPO collapses entropy because the clipped surrogate lets the policy ratio swing too far per step, so the fix is to tighten the clip. The data does not support that framing. DGEntropyGuard, which acts at the sampled-action probability level downstream of the same standardization, barely moves the entropy needle. The variants in the literature that have actually slowed GRPO's entropy decay, DrGRPO and DAPO, get their traction from attacking the standardization instead.

The mechanism lives in the advantage formula. GRPO standardizes rewards within each rollout group, computing `A_i = (R_i - mu) / sigma`. Standardization is a reasonable noise-reduction step when sigma is on the same order as the true reward spread, but on an easy task it is not. Most rollouts in a group agree on the right answer, sigma shrinks toward zero, and the standardized advantage scales as `1/sigma` regardless of how small the raw reward gap `R_i - mu` actually was. A reward gap of 0.01 between two near-identical rollouts produces a standardized advantage of order 1 when sigma is also around 0.01, and the optimizer cannot tell from `A_i` alone that the underlying signal was tiny. The amplification is structural rather than pathological, since standardization is doing exactly what it was designed to do. What the literature on the GRPO entropy problem has, I think, partly missed is how that design interacts with PPO-style clipping.

The PPO clip bounds the policy ratio at `1 +/- eps` per step, which bounds how far one update can move the policy. It does not bound the advantage that drives the update. At the clip boundary the clipped surrogate's gradient is still proportional to `A_i`, and `A_i` is the quantity the standardization has just amplified. Every update therefore saturates the trust region in the direction of whichever rollout happened to win the group, and the saturation continues for as many steps as it takes for entropy to decay below useful levels. The sweep shows entropy crossing 0.1 by step 53 +/- 12, a tighter horizon than the loss curve makes it look, and once entropy is gone the policy stops exploring and the test-error curve flattens regardless of what the clip is doing.

Where the fix lives matters because it changes which interventions are worth running. The clip sits downstream of the standardization, so tightening it constrains the magnitude of each step but neither the direction the step is being pushed in nor the amplification that put it there. DGEntropyGuard sits downstream as well, at the sampled-action probability level, which is consistent with why it barely helps. A fix has to act upstream of the clip. DrGRPO does that by dropping the reward-std normalization in favor of group-centering alone, reducing the sigma-driven amplification at its source, and DAPO does it by decoupling the clipping range so positive and negative updates use different bounds, attacking the asymmetry rather than the standardization itself. Both target the part of the recipe that produces the broken signal in the first place, and on the toy mechanism both should show their advantage by slowing the entropy decay measured here, even before any test-error gap opens up.

### An oracle reviser sources signal, but the oracle is doing the work

The previous section ends on a negative result: token-level credit cannot create gradient where the reward function is silent, so the tool for a sparse-reward task is reward shaping rather than finer routing. Dense correction is the cheapest version of that shaping I could build, and chain-reversal is the task built to test it, since its reward chain places signal only at trajectory level while the underlying task has obvious per-token structure.

The first attempt at this comparison was simply under-budget, which is worth recording because the failure looked like a method result. At 300 steps, exact-match error on chain-reversal cannot discriminate between anything: supervised CE itself finishes near 0.99 error, so no method has room to demonstrate a speedup over a baseline that has not finished either. Extending to 1500 steps makes the question answerable, and the ordering inverts relative to what the short run suggested. SelfDistillDG first reaches zero error around step 467 and SCOPELite around 533, both well ahead of CE at 733. A reviser that converts sparse trajectory reward into dense token supervision is worth roughly 200 to 300 steps against a supervised oracle on this task.

The reason I hold this one at arm's length is that the reviser here is the task's own labels. That makes the experiment a clean test of whether the mechanism can work, and no test at all of whether it works when the reviser is fallible. Every interesting deployment of this idea replaces the oracle with a learned reviser or a process reward model, which introduces exactly the teacher-quality confound the `opd_sandbox` appendix exists to study, and a noisy reviser can plausibly spend its correction budget reinforcing its own errors. So the number I would carry forward is not the 200-step speedup but the shape of the question: dense correction becomes a real lever only if the speedup degrades gracefully as reviser accuracy falls, and that curve is the experiment I have not run.

## Predictions worth testing at scale

The toy runs make two predictions I would want to put to larger experiments. Both are stated so that either outcome is informative, because in each case the toy identifies a mechanism whose transferability is what gets tested, not just an effect to chase.

The first is whether TPO's information-per-step advantage holds up at scale. The gap exists in the toy because grouped rollouts contain more candidate-target information than GRPO's normalization step extracts, and scale pushes on that in two opposing directions. A stronger base policy concentrates its action distribution near the right answer, so the one sampled action already carries most of the signal TPO is currently squeezing out of K candidates, and the gap should close. Richer reward signals, meaning per-token rewards, multi-criterion judges, or per-segment scoring, put more structure into TPO's per-candidate weights, and the gap should widen. The cleanest experiment is a same-task TPO-versus-GRPO comparison with a 1B-class base under fixed rollout cost, which reduces the question to whether the K-candidate extraction is still worth its compute once the base policy is strong. That is where I would put the next budget.

The second is where the entropy-collapse fix for the GRPO family lives. The toy mechanism says the standardization is the load-bearing part of the entropy decay, with the clip downstream of it, and the direct test is an ablation. Hold the PPO clip fixed at its standard `1 +/- eps` band, drop only the reward-std normalization, which recovers the DrGRPO arm, and measure whether the entropy decay slows. If the toy mechanism transfers, the slowdown should hold up across base-model sizes. As a control, tightening the clip without touching the normalization should not slow the entropy decay meaningfully, because the clip is not where the broken signal is being generated. If both ablations slow entropy comparably at scale, the toy diagnosis was wrong and the clip is doing more of the work than the small-scale mechanism suggested, which would itself be useful, because it would localize the disagreement between toy and scale instead of leaving it diffuse.

## Scope

The sandboxes run on a single machine, with CUDA when a GPU is available and CPU otherwise. The methods themselves are scoped to their local batch and task contracts. I keep the part of each published method that decides the update, meaning the update rule, the credit-assignment scheme, and the normalization choices, and drop the distributed-system scaffolding the toy setup does not need. Where the scoping would change a method's meaning rather than only its scale, for instance running advantage normalization in a regime where the original paper assumes a critic, the trainer rejects the config rather than running a variant that would be misleading to compare.

The companions follow the same rule. VPO's local implementation is faithful to the set-reward estimator and explicit about not reproducing the paper's large LM domains. Pedagogical RL keeps privileged teacher GRPO, product-form spike-aware rewards, and surprisal-gated assimilation, without claiming to reproduce the paper's large-model domains. The OPD appendix uses an oracle teacher so that design-choice questions stay clean of teacher-quality confounds. The learnability sandbox is the one that reaches outside, since its chess results are computed against released checkpoints and datasets pinned at fixed revisions, which its README documents.

Distributed rollout, learned critics, and production reward pipelines are out of scope by design. They cost the observability the sandbox is built around, and observability is what the small scale buys.
